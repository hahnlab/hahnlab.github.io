Research

Exploring organismal function and evolution through genomic data

Gene Gain & Loss Population Genomics Speciation Genomics Transcriptional Regulation

Our work focuses broadly on asking questions about organismal function and evolution using genomic data. The huge amount of data currently being produced allows us to ask and answer questions on a genomic scale that have never been possible before. Our questions largely revolve around the relative roles of natural selection and genetic drift in shaping nucleotide, gene family, and gene expression variation both within and between species.

Although most of the empirical work has been on systems such as humans, flies and mosquitoes (and now tomatoes!), members of the lab can work on topics and organisms that appeal to them. This page covers several major topics currently being studied.

The Evolution of Gene Gain and Loss

Comparison of whole genomes has revealed large and frequent changes in the size of gene families— the result of gene duplication and loss. Comparative genomic analyses allow us to identify large-scale patterns of change and to make inferences regarding the role of natural selection in gene gain and loss.

To make these analyses possible, we have developed a stochastic birth-and-death model for gene family evolution, applied in the software package CAFE. Application of this method to data from multiple whole genomes of many groups is revealing remarkable patterns of gene gain and loss.

Other approaches to studying this question involve analyzing gene movement among chromosomes (especially sex chromosomes), discovering polymorphic copy-number variants under local selection, and developing methods for genome assembly to more accurately estimate gene number.

Population Genomics

Selective, demographic, and random processes all determine the frequency of alleles in a population and differences between species. One of the major goals of population genetics is to uncover which of these processes is acting in natural populations through a combination of empirical studies and theoretical models.

With genomic-scale data, the field now requires new approaches. We explore these questions in studies focused largely on humans and flies. Our work includes new methods for distinguishing demography from selection, detecting different forms of positive selection, and using clinal variation to uncover local adaptation.

Speciation Genomics

Population genomic data are being applied in new ways to recently diverged lineages. In the emerging field of speciation genomics, a key goal is understanding how patterns of divergence relate to mechanisms of reproductive isolation.

Focusing on divergence within the Anopheles gambiae species complex, we investigate the roles of introgression and selection in shaping heterogeneous patterns of divergence. Our work includes new approaches to detecting introgression and distinguishing differences among loci.

The Evolution of Transcriptional Regulation

Changes in the timing, level, and location of gene expression contribute to phenotypic differences between individuals and species. Using DNA sequence and gene expression data, we study the origins of expression variation and the evolutionary forces acting on it.

We focus on the evolution of transcription factor binding sites and their influence on expression. With the rise of RNA-seq, we frequently begin by observing expression differences. Much of our recent work uses wild tomatoes from the genus Solanum, leveraging genetic manipulation to gain deeper insights into transcriptional divergence.
